Yuki Lea-Foster

Bjorn in Japan, lived there for first 13 years, then moved to Hawaii, and has since moved to AR.


I asked Yuki nine questions, but she had more to say than that. For the first questions I asked, they were to get to know the overall environment better, helping me understand the question of faith and truth. For some things, she had extra thoughts to contribute that didn't always directly relate to the questions asked, but still provided cultural insight. Also note that this is all from her own personal experience with these areas, so this may not be generalizable to everyone, or even most people in Japan or Hawaii.

The first question is, "What American cultural norms do you find different from Japan (or Hawaii)?" To this, she answered that in Japan, social norms are very different. Everything is all about appearance, how others think about you, and being very polite to everybody. She described the politeness like the language you use in an essay: it's very formal; insincere, but dressed up nicely; they use fake smiles, and she remarks that there was very much a wall between her and the people around her.

The second and third questions are, "what are your thoughts on Japanese stereotypes" and "are there some that you find humerus?" She opined that the common belief that everybody watches and likes anime is not really accurate, but more like American football: not every American knows everything about it, or likes it. Another was that everybody likes sushi.

In question four, "what foods do you miss from both islands [Japan and Hawaii]?" Yuki said she missed the fresh fish, and other fresh foods there, partly because they were cheaper there. She remembers her local town giving gifts of food, laundry detergent, and other things that are nice, or helpful in the hot season. This form of gift giving was for gratitude, and good wishes of health; like saying "thank you for your hospitality." This was a mix between a local, Japanese, and Buddhist thing, and her family would give fruit. She also misses the Japan gummies, which she remarked that she could taste and smell the richness of the fruit, and that they seemed less artificial in those same regards. As another thought, she misses the public transportation, and being able to go anywhere and everywhere safely.

In question five I ask, "how does living on an island differ from living in a land locked state [like Arkansas]?" Even though fresh fish, and other fruit, and other fresh foods are cheaper, most things are pretty expensive on the islands, because they're imported. For instance, gas prices at the moment we talked were $5.57/gal. So people often used electric cars; everywhere had a place to charge an electric car, but here, you don't really see much of those unless you're looking for them.

I asked for question six, "how is socializing different (general feel of talking to people; is it more difficult, or are there certain social aspects that are/not present compared to the main 48 states and U.S.)?" To this, she said Hawaii is very friendly, relaxed, there's not really any pressure for anything. For Japan, she said it's not like that at all. It's the "yes ma'am; yes sir" culture; there's constant manners to your superiors. However, I asked what Japanese words they would use to refer to their superiors specifically, and she could not recall any, half-jokingly saying, "as long as you don't cuss at them, I think." At hearing this, I asked, "which felt more like home to you?" To which, she replied that Hawaii felt more like home than Japan, because she wasn't really accepted for who she was in Japan, and didn't feel that she belonged, having her own opinions, and life-style. She remembered that she always hung out around people in lower grades than hers, because she felt more needed than the people in her own grade. She commented that you couldn't really talk about opinions in Japan, or different values. The stronger and more popular opinions, and values were the ones that everybody had to pretend to agree with, at least somewhat. In her experience, people would "disappear from [her]" or exclude her when she had differing opinions and values from others. She felt that she couldn't be who she really was in Japan. Afterwards, she mentioned that in America (or at least here in Arkansas) that people are more faith based on God, and that he created everyone in His image, and everybody loves each other; but in Japan, it's not like that. She added that you can't say "no" in Japan, or it's considered "selfish."

That really goes well with her answer to question seven, "What are some things that people don't really think about in Japan that is different from America?" She said that you can't think about yourself, and it's all about everybody else--you're not really allowed to think about yourself. From what she's seen, to provide, a lot of people will continue working very late in life, and often to death, working their butt off. 

Yuki's parents are flying back to Japan, so I asked "How do they act in Japan compared to America?" Her father goes on business trips in Japan, which are different from American ones. What often happens socially is instead of taking people out to eat, and just having a good time like in America, they involve a lot of alcohol. What's more is the peer pressure is from your boss, who can withhold a promotion if you don't go. This is what she referred to as "drinking harassment." This is another case where you can't really say "no."

For the ninth and final question, I asked, "What are some differences in faith, and perspectives of what truth is?" In Hawaii, there were a lot of Non-Denominational or Catholics, where as in Japan, there were Buddhists, Shinto, and people who weren't religious. Yuki went to Pearl Harbor Church of Christ, which is right outside of the base that was bombed, and gets roughly 50 people on a good day.

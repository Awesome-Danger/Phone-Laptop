I asked Yuki nine questions, but she had more to say than that. For the first questions I asked, they were to get to know the overall environment better, helping me understand the question of faith and truth. For some things, she had extra thoughts to contribute that didn't always directly relate to the questions asked, but still provided cultural insight. Also note that this is all from her own personal experience with these areas, so this may not be generalizable to everyone, or even most people in Japan or Hawaii.

The first question I asked is, "What American cultural norms do you find different from Japan (or Hawaii)?" To this, she answered that in Japan, social norms are indeed very different. Everything is all about appearance, how others think about you, and being very **polite** to everybody. She described the politeness like the language you use in an essay: it's very formal; insincere, but dressed up nicely; they use fake smiles, and she remarks that there was very much a wall between her and the people around her. In Japan, you always **belittle** yourself, and make yourself out to be small and insignificant. Fir instance, if someone asks how big your house is, no matter how big your house is, you would say something like, "Oh, I live in a cage, it's tiny." She said that you **can't think about yourself,** and it's all about everybody else--you're not really allowed to think about yourself. From what she's seen, to provide, a lot of people will continue working very late in life, and often to death, working their butt off.  Yuki's parents are flying back to Japan, so I asked "How do they act in Japan compared to America?" Her father goes on business trips in Japan, which are different from American ones. What often happens socially is instead of taking people out to eat, and just having a good time like in America, they involve a lot of alcohol. What's more is the peer pressure is from your boss, who can withhold a promotion if you don't go. This is what she referred to as **"drinking harassment."** This is another case where you can't really say "no."

How is **socializing** different, as in general feel of talking to people; is it more difficult, or are there certain social aspects that are/not present compared to the main 48 states and U.S.? To this, she said Hawaii is very friendly, relaxed, there's not really any pressure for anything. For Japan, she said it's not like that at all. It's the "yes ma'am; yes sir" culture; there's constant manners to your superiors. However, I asked what Japanese words they would use to refer to their superiors specifically, and she could not recall any, half-jokingly saying, "as long as you don't cuss at them, I think." At hearing this, I asked, "which felt more like home to you?" To which, she replied that Hawaii felt more like home than Japan, because she wasn't really accepted for who she was in Japan, and didn't feel that she belonged, having her own opinions, and life-style. She remembered that she always hung out around people in lower grades than hers, because she felt more needed than the people in her own grade. She commented that you couldn't really talk about opinions in Japan, or different values. The stronger and more popular opinions, and values were the ones that everybody had to pretend to agree with, at least somewhat. In her experience, people would disappear, or exclude her when she had differing opinions and values from others. She felt that she couldn't be who she really was in Japan. Afterwards, she mentioned that in America (or at least here in Arkansas) that people are more faith based on God, and that he created everyone in His image, and everybody loves each other; but in Japan, it's not like that. She added that you can't say "no" in Japan, or it's considered "selfish."

I asked her thoughts on Japanese stereotypes and if there some that she finds humerus. She opined that the common belief that everybody watches and likes **anime** is not really accurate, but more like American football: not every American knows everything about it, or likes it. Another was that everybody likes sushi.

I asked Yuki what foods she you misses from both islands (Japan and Hawaii). She missed the fresh fish, and other fresh foods there, partly because they were cheaper there. She remembers her local town giving gifts of food, laundry detergent, and other things that are nice, or helpful in the hot season. This form of gift giving was for gratitude, and good wishes of health; like saying "thank you for your hospitality." This was a mix between a local, Japanese, and Buddhist thing, and her family would give **fruit**. She also misses the Japan gummies, which she remarked that she could taste and smell the richness of the fruit, and that they seemed less artificial in those same regards. As another thought, she misses the public transportation, and being able to go anywhere and everywhere safely. How does living on an island differ from living in a land locked state like Arkansas? Even though fresh fish, and other fruit, and other fresh foods are cheaper, most things are pretty expensive on the islands, because they're imported. For instance, gas prices at the moment we talked were $5.57/gal. So people often used electric cars; everywhere had a place to charge an electric car, but here, you don't really see much of those unless you're looking for them.

In Japan there are significant differences in **faith**, and truth. In Hawaii, there were a lot of Non-Denominational or Catholics, where as in Japan, there were Buddhists, Shinto, and people who weren't religious. In Kindergarten through 6th grade, she was taught in a catholic school. While in Hawaii, Yuki went to Pearl Harbor Church of Christ, which is right outside of the base that was bombed, and gets roughly 50 people on a good day, with Yuki and her family were the only Japanese family there, and had started attending in roughly 2015-2020. The people there were friendly, and Yuki felt more involved in the church there. While we talked on faith and the Japanese perspective on truth, Yuki wasn't sure, but going off of what she had said earlier, we both agree that it's likely what that majority of people say.

With this, the topic of **male and female roles** in marriage came up. She mentioned that women do everything at home: cooking and serving food, cleaning, taking care of the kids, and so on, all without questioning the husband, and with complete obedience. Women also have strange beauty standards. In Yuki's experience, they were never taught, to wear make-up, and were not expected or encouraged to either, but as soon as they turned a certain age, they were expected to be wearing the right amount of make-up to show respect, and it's disrespectful not to. You also can't wear too many piercings, or have dyed hair, or people will think you're no doing well, or a hooker or in a gang. For men, they HAVE to be tough. Even while a family is grieving, the man is not to show any emotion. In America, it's always ladies first, but not so in Japan. For example, during Valentine's Day, the woman gives the man a gift, and exactly a month later, if he is interested, gives it back to her; the women have to put in all the effort.

I can glean from my encounter with Yuki that Japan is definitely a place with more social tension between each other, but I also know that it's not an unpleasant place for travelers. I know it's a very clean, and tidy place. which further compounds onto the external image that Japan wants to give off. As for what I learned about myself, is that there's a theme that keeps coming up when it comes to a couple of my classes, and that's external image versus the inner self. That could also be because I'm a psychology major, and that's something I'm going to have to cover for the rest of my life, but this still reminds me that I need to be careful about the difference between my external, and internal self, and to be careful that there isn't much of a difference. A second thing I think I'm learning is that I feel this would be a place that I might like to do a long-term mission trip in, if I were to decide I'm willing to learn yet another language, and to not let my roots go too deep here in America. Yuki moved from Japan, to Hawaii, to Searcy, Arkansas. Those are three wildly different places, all with their own cultures, subcultures, weather, etc. 

Most importantly, however, I am learning more and more about what kind of life would be ahead of me if I were to do missions. I love learning about people, whether it's their minds, culture, history (depend on what type of history), and folk lore, I love learning about people. In an ideal world, I would like to live as a missionary, or spread the word outward in some way or form to groups of people that don't know God like I do. This encounter is showing me that some countries are not very accepting of new or different ideas in general, and that's something that I would need to fight. However, Yuki had also told me a bit about her missionary English teacher, and her husband (he had mostly taught her English). Yuki informed me they were very respectful of her views (I think that's to contrast how the other people in Japan didn't), and they showed Jesus in their actions and love. This makes me think of the missionary English teacher I heard of in the All Missions Fellowship meet, who had taught the Albanian missionary we spoke with. She did something very similar, and hadn't pushed anything onto him when he said that he only wanted to learn English, and didn't want to read the Bible, but ended up being converted through socializing with fellow students at the church that he was invited to by her. It's taught me that I need to remember that sometimes, no matter how hard I try, it's *not* trying that will be the most effective, if I am truly portraying Christ merely in my common, everyday actions.
